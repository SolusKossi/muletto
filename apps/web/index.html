Your files never leave this device. There is no upload, no account and no server that could receive them - the browser is told to refuse it. What is stored, and where

They have to give you your data. Nobody said it had to arrive readable.

Ask Apple, Google, Meta or Snapchat for everything they hold on you and a few zip files turn up. Inside: numbered folders, machine-written JSON, your messages split across services, years of locations you did not know were kept, and the same photograph four times over with the dates stripped out.

Muletto opens the lot in this tab and hands back one thing you can read, search and keep.

Reading the export is the easy half

Messages and records only have to be made legible. The pictures are a different job. There are (usually) tens of thousands, spread across every archive, most with their dates gone and plenty of them saved more than once.

Duplicates found across services

Any photo tool spots two copies in the same folder. The ones filling your disk sit in four different archives under four filenames with four different dates, and nothing else finds them because nothing else opens more than one export at a time.

Muletto compares the contents, so the same picture from iCloud, Google, Snapchat and a WhatsApp re-save is one picture. It catches the near-copies too: the burst, the crop, the screenshot of the screenshot.

The work goes into the files, not an account

Dates and places are written into the photographs themselves, in the fields every photo application already reads. Move the library to Apple Photos, to Immich, to a NAS or to a drive in a drawer and it stays sorted and searchable.

Nothing is held here. There is no account to lose and no library that stops working if this site does.

What happens, in order, from asking to keeping

One pass through the whole thing, from asking a service for your data to clearing it away again. Each step says what happens and why it works that way.

  1. You ask them for it

    Every service has to hand over what it holds on you, and every one of them has buried the button somewhere different. The guides walk each request, screen by screen, and say what the wait usually is.

    Muletto is not involved. You are asking them directly, with your own account, and the archive arrives at your machine.

  2. You open it here

    The zip's index is read from the end of the file, and entries are inflated one at a time with DecompressionStream over File.slice(). The archive is never held whole, so a 50 GB Takeout behaves like a small one. Archives past 2 GB arrive in a different format again, and that is read here too.

    Nothing is sent anywhere, and the page is served with a Content-Security-Policy whose connect-src lets the browser refuse it on our behalf. Turn off your wifi and every step below still runs.

  3. It is read into one library

    Photographs, messages, location history, searches, watch history, payments and account tables, out of whatever shape each service chose - Snapchat's JSON, Meta's per-thread files, Google's per-product folders, Apple's and Samsung's CSVs - merged onto one timeline by the timestamps they already carry.

    Two services that logged the same afternoon end up next to each other, which you cannot see while they sit in separate folders.

  4. Small copies are made, once

    Each picture is shrunk to a 320px copy and written to your browser's own storage, on disk. Videos give up their first frame the same way.

    A wall of three thousand photographs cannot open the originals every time you scroll - that is megabytes each. The copies are made once, in background threads so the page stays alive, and are still there next visit and when the same photograph turns up inside next year's export.

  5. Duplicates are found by content

    Every file is keyed by a SHA-256 of its bytes. The archive's own checksum and length are a free first pass that only suggests a match; nothing is treated as the same photograph until the hash agrees. Near-copies are caught separately with a perceptual hash, with the flat images held back because a blank screenshot has no fingerprint worth comparing.

    The copies wasting your disk are in four different archives under four filenames with four dates. Only content finds those.

  6. Dates and places are put back

    Recovered from the sidecar JSON that Google and Meta ship beside each file, or from the container for video, then written into the JPEG as EXIF DateTimeOriginal and a GPS block - rebuilt whole rather than spliced, because inserting a tag shifts every offset after it.

    A half-correct EXIF loses the date and moves the location, which is worse than leaving it alone.

  7. You write it out and keep it

    Straight to a folder through the File System Access API where the browser has it, or one streamed archive where it does not - compressed as it is written, so only the index is held. Descriptions go in as XMP dc:description, which Lightroom, digiKam, Immich, Bridge and Apple Photos all read.

    Ordinary folders and ordinary files. Move them to a NAS or a drive in a drawer and they stay sorted and searchable with nothing of ours involved.

  8. You forget this ever happened

    We hold nothing to delete - there is no account, no server and no copy of your data anywhere but your own machine. What is left after a session is a small pile of working files in your own browser: the comparisons, the repaired dates and the little thumbnail copies. Harmless, and yours.

    One button clears them, after saying what goes and offering to save the work to a file first. Take that option and your exported folders are all that remains; take the button and there is no trace left of any of this - not with us, because there never was any, and not on your machine either.

These are read too: Reddit, Spotify, X, Discord, Strava, TikTok, WhatsApp, Fitbit, LinkedIn, Microsoft and Amazon. Those readers are written from what each service documents and none of them has met a real export yet, which is a weaker thing and is said as such on every one of their guides.

Two honest limits. Writing a date back needs JPEG - other formats are copied untouched and reported, not silently skipped. And folder writing needs showDirectoryPicker, which today means Chrome or Edge; Safari and Firefox get one streamed archive instead.
